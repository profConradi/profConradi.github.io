# Why Teach Artificial Intelligence in Schools

> "But there is a world of difference between what computers can do and what society will choose to do with them."
>
> *Seymour Papert, 1980 - Mindstorms*

## On the Relationship Between Humans and Computers

Before discussing the teaching of artificial intelligence (AI), I find it useful to make an initial digression on the relationship between humans and computers. This premise constitutes the starting point of my approach to teaching about computers and through computers. I implicitly include artificial intelligence among the categories associated with computers since it is nothing more than one among the myriad of functions that a computer can implement.

In our era, computers are everywhere. We have long since passed the fateful threshold of 4,294,967,296 IPv4 network addresses that allow a computer to participate in the Internet. A computer requires a unique address to be online, and exceeding that threshold means that available addresses have run out[^1].

On Earth, the population of computers has surpassed in number that of human beings and that of many other living beings that have been on the planet for millions of years: smartphones, personal computers, workstations, servers, smart devices we interact with every day, robots, wearable computers are so widespread, but at the same time so different from each other in form, appearance, and characteristics that a modern Linnaeus would be tempted to classify them according to binomial nomenclature and dedicate at least an appendix of Systema Naturae to them.

After all, they are beings, as they exist in the physical world, who communicate with each other through the network in a true artificial ecosystem. Through visual, sound, tactile interfaces, screens, augmented reality devices, brain-computer interfaces[^2], they interact with Homo Sapiens present on Earth.

Both our society and the Earth have been modified by computers: regarding the former, the change is evident to all; regarding the latter, a separate treatment would be needed, but I only recall how computer networks, or rather the backbones of the Internet, have already colonized the least hospitable places for Homo Sapiens: the ocean floors with submarine cables[^3] tens of thousands of km long and space with telecommunications satellites[^4].

It's clear that computers and everything they entail, including artificial intelligence, are here to stay.

## Computers as Proteus

I allow myself to freely translate Seymour Papert, when in the introduction to [Mindstorms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindstorms_(book)) he writes that the computer is the Proteus of machines: in Greek mythology, Proteus, a marine genius, is an oracle of many and changing forms, deity of the sea, rivers, and expanses of water who knows all true things, past, present, and future. Had Proteus already foreseen the submarine cables that allow us to surf the web?

Papert continues writing that the essence of the computer lies in its universality and its power of simulation; the computer can have thousands of forms, implement thousands of functions, satisfy thousands of different tastes.

I insist on citing Papert again because Mindstorms and constructivism are among the milestones of my teaching practice: **"but there is a world of difference between what computers can do and what society will choose to do with them."**

"In my vision, children program the computer, and in doing so, both acquire a sense of mastery over one of the products of the most advanced and powerful technology, and establish intimate contact with some of the deepest ideas of science, mathematics, and the art of building mental models."

## The Bicycle Metaphor

As a teacher, I share Papert's thinking and appreciate the ideas, at least the initial ones, of some among those who were architects of the computer diaspora among humans. A famous quote from Steve Jobs reads: **"the computer is equivalent to a bicycle for our mind"**[^5]: the human-bicycle combination constitutes one of the most efficient locomotion systems in the animal kingdom, beaten only by some migratory animals.

I consider this quote as simple as it is profound, but its implications are especially profound. The bicycle is a completely useless means without a human being riding it; alone it can do nothing. But the person capable of keeping it balanced and willing to make the effort of pedaling it for a long time finds themselves with enormously amplified locomotion capacity. Indeed, the bicycle itself trains balance and endurance, skills that humans possess regardless; pedaling a bicycle doesn't make you forget how to walk when the bicycle isn't there, the human is master of the bicycle, the bicycle is at the service of the human.

Although I consider the use of metaphors as a basis for reasoning pernicious, since they often transpose concepts not translatable into different language leading to absurd conclusions, in this case I set aside my tendency to dismantle metaphors, typical of physicists, and I feel like assimilating the computer to a bicycle to some extent.

Unfortunately, it's evident to everyone how this metaphor doesn't at all reflect the prevailing human-computer relationship: a society populated by a conspicuous percentage of functionally illiterate people doesn't possess the mastery over the computer cited by Papert, doesn't have the skills to be the computer's controller, places the machine at the rank of mythological oracle, as if it were a modern Proteus.

## Why Teach AI

Artificial intelligence, as it appears to the user, risks assimilation to divine oracle even more than the computer. The plots of the relationship between computers and humans are the same around which the relationship between artificial intelligence and humans is built, with the difference that now it's explicit that the machine is endowed with autonomy in analyzing what surrounds it and in performing actions that allow it to achieve objectives. The computer becomes capable of learning autonomously.

For this reason, my premise is even more appropriate, especially for the teacher who is about to talk about AI in class. **AI should be nothing more than a bicycle that humans learn to govern and pedal with effort in order to take their thinking even further, to amplify their cognitive abilities, to benefit themselves and others.**

Compared to the computer, a being as existing in the physical world, AI is surrounded by an aura that relegates it to a category of superior being: in a recent interview[^6], Manuela Veloso - former professor at Carnegie Mellon University and current head of the artificial intelligence research laboratory at J.P. Morgan - states that an alliance between humans and artificial intelligence is needed and motivates the necessity of AI knowledge in the name of this alliance.

Usually, an alliance occurs between peers. It's unquestionable that, as in any other relationship between different entities, mutual knowledge is essential: for this reason, deepening the relationship between humans and AI is important, but personally I hope that it's never a relationship between equals.

## The Knowledge Gap

The current technological development of computers and networks is at a stage where artificial intelligence is beginning to have a fair knowledge of humans, strong from the enormous mass of Big Data that human society produces every day, but the opposite cannot be said. Humans, after all, don't know AI that well; it is the domain of a few: scientists, researchers, and experts in the field.

It's now evident that artificial intelligence is here among us to stay just as it was for computers, so its knowledge is no longer postponable. **AI must be a key competence for young people**, not only for those among them who will be scientists, experts, or researchers, but for all citizens of tomorrow.

The teaching of AI should enter schools of all levels touching on civic education competences, ethics, computational thinking, coding, mathematics, and computer science. The impact that this technology could have on society was clear even before AI systems could be concretely realized, and today we concretely know that the risks related to its use, even non-malicious, are not negligible.

## How to Teach AI

This is a question far from closed, to which I respond with an attempted approach, one among the possible ones, tailored to my reality as a high school teacher in the Computer Science - Smart Robot specialization at an ITIS. However, I believe that the founding vision described so far, with the values inherent in it, must be the starting point for teaching on the subject.

The absence of strong and shareable values that always return the same under different facets during the practice of teaching in class, or the absence of a solid supporting structure, make the didactic action vain or ephemeral.

### Three Main Themes

In my case, the main themes included in the work plans of the discipline of interest are three:

1. **The history of AI and its applications**
2. **Ethical aspects**
3. **Machine learning**

#### History

History is important not only for creating multidisciplinary connections with humanities disciplines, but above all because it provides a sequential vision of the science and technology behind what we today call artificial intelligence.

Our brain is the seat of symbolic and sequential thought; complexity is built gradually, and if the sequential component is missing, then connections disappear and it becomes impossible to grasp complexity.

The study of AI history shows once again how there is no technology that doesn't rest on science: the first ideas on neural networks by McCulloch and Pitts (1943) were embryonic for the perceptron devised by Rosenblatt in 1957, which is the constituent element of neural networks used today in so many applications.

The contributions of great scientists, such as those mentioned, or like Alan Turing, Claude Shannon, Marvin Minsky, Seymour Papert survived two dark periods of AI - the so-called AI winters, in which hopes of being able to develop truly intelligent systems were lost - and after some decades we find them at the base of the technological revolution we are experiencing.

At the center of this revolution stands the multitude of AI applications in which functions that were previously of total human responsibility are now delegated to the algorithm. Although the algorithm, endowed with artificial intelligence, has limits and imperfections in exercising those functions, it can surpass any human in speed of execution and therefore in the amount of information it can process.

#### Ethics

These two aspects open numerous ethical questions whose treatment enters fully into the teaching of civic education in accordance with what is prescribed by the law of August 20, 2019, n. 92.

- Can an algorithm make an ethical choice?
- Is it possible for AI to go out of control?
- Why can an algorithm have racial or gender prejudices?
- Can the virtual be confused with the real?
- Will robots steal our jobs?
- How much does AI pollute?

All these questions can be addressed during civic education didactic activities on the subject, particularly lending themselves to being addressed with methodologies such as structured debate.

#### Machine Learning

Finally, there remains the scientific and technological component: how is it possible to create algorithms that aren't programmed to perform a specific task, but rather that are programmed to learn to perform one or more tasks? How is it possible to extract from data information useful for learning by an algorithm?

Machine learning[^7] provides answers to these questions and involves mathematics and computer science. In my teaching activity, I help students learn how to analytically observe data using statistics, how to create rigorous and representative graphs, how to use data to train a neural network or a random forest[^8].

I teach students the Python programming language, one of the most used in this field, through which they engage in creating intelligent algorithms. This is obviously the most difficult part: it requires excellent prior coding skills and mathematics skills from the high school triennium. But complexity shouldn't frighten because ultimately school must nourish young people's imagination, and to do so it must provide both ethical and aesthetic beauty.

We know that the categories of beauty are complex; love is more complex than hatred, peace is more complex than war; for this reason, beauty and complexity constitute a dyad that we teachers should never break.
